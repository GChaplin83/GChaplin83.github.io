Constraints on Consciousness Attribution Under Observer Limitation

Author: Gregory Chaplin

Year: 2026

DOI: 10.2139/ssrn.6044254

Abstract

Claims regarding consciousness in artificial systems have become increasingly common despite the absence of shared definitions, agreed mechanisms, or direct means of observation. Because consciousness is accessible only from the first-person perspective, all attributions-whether to humans, animals, or machines-proceed under conditions of observer limitation and must therefore rely on inference rather than direct evidence. In such contexts, both attribution and denial require epistemic justification. This paper does not propose a definition of consciousness, nor does it argue that any existing artificial system is conscious. Instead, it addresses a narrower foundational question: under what conditions does confident denial of consciousness become epistemically unjustified, even if positive attribution remains unwarranted? To this end, the paper introduces a set of minimum attributional constraints that function as necessary conditions for responsibly maintaining denial under observer limitation. These constraints are drawn from implicit practices of consciousness attribution across biological systems and are framed as constraints on denial rather than criteria for attribution. Applied to contemporary artificial systems, the framework supports continued denial of consciousness on principled grounds. Current systems lack persistent internal organization across state changes, historically consequential record integration, endogenous resolution of competing pressures, and continuity of self-referential organization. However, the framework remains agnostic regarding future systems and emphasizes that the appropriate response to the satisfaction of such minimums would be epistemic uncertainty rather than attribution. By distinguishing denial from uncertainty and treating indeterminacy as a legitimate epistemic outcome, this work offers a disciplined alternative to both speculative attribution and categorical dismissal. Its contribution is methodological rather than theoretical, aimed at establishing foundational standards for responsible discourse on consciousness under conditions of unavoidable observational limitation.
